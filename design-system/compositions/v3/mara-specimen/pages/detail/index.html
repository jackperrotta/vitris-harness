01 / The question

What must a visitor know at the threshold?

A directory can list every room and still leave a person unsure where to go. The first decision in Field Notes is to separate arrival, movement and destination. Each has a different job and a different moment of attention.

The invented brief has three destinations: entrance, reading room and courtyard. The specimen gives each a short name and a consistent directional relationship. It is a teaching example of hierarchy, not tested public signage.

02 / The hierarchy

Make one decision visible at a time.

The largest words establish the immediate question. Smaller destination labels hold the practical answer. The space between them is part of the system: it keeps the reader from treating everything as equally urgent.

DestinationReading room →Short label / consistent direction / room to see it

Type scale, line breaks and contrast work together. A mobile view cannot borrow the distance of a wall sign, so the specimen reduces scale and preserves the order instead of squeezing the desktop composition into a narrow strip.

03 / The reading

Follow the sequence, then revise.

The useful test is a reader’s path: what do they notice first, what question follows and where do they act? This concept makes that sequence inspectable. It does not claim measured navigation success or compliance with a real building’s signage requirements.

In a commissioned project, language, route names, sightlines and accessibility would need verification with the people who use the space.

02 / Open Table / Publication study / 2026

The same care,
another rhythm.

A publication gives the reader more time. Open Table explores the quieter hierarchy of heading, running text and caption. The rule stays useful: establish a way through, then let the material develop.

White space is not a pause in the work.
